Capitals that moved

Every time a state picked up its seat of government and put it down somewhere else since 1500, as far as the open record goes: from Moscow to a new city on the Neva, from Lisbon across the Atlantic to Rio de Janeiro, from Rio to a plateau where Brasília did not yet exist. How far each went, which way, and how many capitals were built from nothing.

Dr Tristan Hotham · Independent researcher · 2026 · Data: Wikidata, checked and corrected by hand; Natural Earth

Most capitals feel permanent. London, Paris and Beijing have been where they are for so long that it is hard to imagine them anywhere else. But capitals move more often than you might think. Brazil built Brasília in four years and moved there in 1960; Nigeria left Lagos for Abuja in 1991; Myanmar's government packed up for Naypyidaw, a city it had built in secret, in 2005. And in January 2026 Equatorial Guinea moved its capital from Malabo, on an island, to Ciudad de la Paz on the mainland.

I wanted to see all of these on one map. Wikidata, the open database behind Wikipedia, records the capitals of thousands of states with the years each was the capital, so it should be possible to draw every move as an arc from the old capital to the new. It is, but only after a lot of checking: the dates are patchy, the same move is often recorded on several items, and some of the most famous moves are missing altogether. I checked the famous ones against cited sources, corrected what was wrong, added what was missing, and wrote every change down. The result is 184 moves since 1500 by 114 states. Press play, drag the slider, or tap an arc for its story.

    02 · WhenA restless 20th century

    The 20th century saw more moves than any other: 59, of which 44 lasted. Some were born of war and revolution: the Bolsheviks left Petrograd for Moscow in 1918, Atatürk's republic chose Ankara over Istanbul in 1923, and the Republic of China retreated from Nanjing to Chongqing in 1937 and, in 1949, through Guangzhou, Chongqing and Chengdu to Taipei. The busiest decade of all is the 1940s, with 18 moves, most of them wartime. Others were deliberate: Canberra, Brasília, Islamabad, Abuja.

    The earlier centuries look quieter partly because they were, and partly because they are less well recorded. Wikidata knows a great deal about the European states of the 16th to 18th centuries, down to small German principalities that moved their courts a few kilometres to a new palace, and much less about Africa, Asia and the Americas before the colonial era. Read the counts as moves that the record knows about.

    03 · How farFrom across the street to across an ocean

    Most moves are short. The median lasting move is 177 km, and 36% of lasting moves covered less than 100 km: Thonburi to Bangkok across the Chao Phraya in 1782 (4 km), Durlach to the new palace city of Karlsruhe in 1715. But a few crossed continents and oceans.

    The longest moves since 1500

    Great-circle distance from the old capital to the new. Dashed: a temporary seat. Hover for the story.

    The record holder is the Portuguese court's flight from Napoleon's army in 1807: the royal family, the government and thousands of courtiers sailed from Lisbon and set up in Rio de Janeiro in 1808, 7,716 km away. For thirteen years a European empire was ruled from the Americas; King João VI went home in 1821. The longest lasting move is the Sultan of Oman's: in 1840 Said bin Sultan moved his capital from Muscat to Stone Town on Zanzibar, 3,924 km down the coast of Africa, the heart of his trading empire. Next come Chengdu to Taipei (1,828 km) and Calcutta to Delhi (1,305 km).

    04 · Built from nothingCapitals made to order

    17 of the moves went to a capital that was built, or laid out as a new town, for the purpose. The idea is old: Peter the Great founded St Petersburg in 1703 and moved his court there from Moscow in 1712, and the margraves of Baden laid out Karlsruhe in 1715 as a fan of streets around their palace. Washington was planned from 1791. But 12 of the 17 came after 1900, when a new capital became a statement of nationhood: New Delhi, Canberra, Brasília, Islamabad, Belmopan, Gaborone, Abuja, Naypyidaw.

    Away from the coast

    Many modern moves went inland. Of the 48 lasting moves since 1900, 13 (27%) left a capital within 50 km of the sea for one at least 50 km further inland, against 8% of the moves before 1900. Rio to Brasília, Lagos to Abuja, Dar es Salaam to Dodoma, Yangon to Naypyidaw, Abidjan to Yamoussoukro, Karachi to Islamabad, Malabo to Ciudad de la Paz: a colonial port gives way to a capital in the interior, often closer to the country's middle. Only one went the other way: the Republic of China's flight to Taipei. Of 45 lasting moves since 1900 within one present-day country, 25 brought the capital at least 25 km closer to the centre of the country's main landmass; Brasília is 916 km closer to Brazil's centre than Rio.

    05 · Who moved mostThe restless and the settled

    Counted by present-day country, the United States has the most moves: 17, but only 5 of them lasting. Eleven belong to the young republic itself: the Continental Congress fled the British to Baltimore, Lancaster and York, was driven out of Philadelphia by unpaid soldiers in 1783, and sat in Princeton, Annapolis, Trenton and New York before settling on Philadelphia and then Washington in 1800. The rest are the Kingdom of Hawaiʻi and the Republic of Texas. Germany is next with 12, from 8 different states, most of them small German courts; then India and China (10 each), Russia and France (9).

    At the other end, 81% of present-day sovereign states have only one capital in Wikidata. That is true for some (Copenhagen has been Denmark's capital for centuries) but it also hides a great deal: Wikidata does not record, for example, that Belize moved from Belize City, that the Philippines spent 28 years with Quezon City as its capital, or that Turkey's capital was Istanbul until 1923. I added those by hand, but there will be many more.

    The longest move of a capital since 1500 was not a planned new city but a flight: the Portuguese court, 7,716 km from Lisbon to Rio de Janeiro.

    07 · Still movingNusantara, and a new capital in 2026

    The newest move in the data is Equatorial Guinea's, in 2026: a presidential decree reported in January moved the capital 331 km from Malabo, on the island of Bioko, to Ciudad de la Paz, a planned city in the mainland forest, and gave the government a year to relocate. Egypt's New Administrative Capital and South Korea's Sejong City, which house ministries but are not the official capital, are not counted.

    08 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    The data. I took every capital (P36) statement in Wikidata for items that are countries, sovereign states, historical countries, kingdoms, realms, republics, empires and constitutional monarchies, with the statement's start and end years, its rank, the capital's coordinates, and the state's founding and end dates: 2,978 statements for 2,295 states. Only 21.3% of statements have a start or an end year. I kept states that still existed after 1500.

    Reading moves. A move is read where one capital's time starts and another's ends within two years; or where a new capital starts and the old one has no end date; or where the old one ends and a new one with no start date runs on. Capitals that overlap for more than two years are treated as joint capitals, not a move. Deprecated statements and "unknown value" capitals are left out. States with several capitals but no dates at all (87 of them) cannot be placed in time and are not counted. Renamings and redefinitions of the same city (Edo to Tokyo, Constantinople to Istanbul, Buda to Budapest) are not moves. The same move recorded on several items (Russia's Tsardom and Empire, Portugal's Kingdom and Empire) is counted once. This gave 202 candidate moves since 1500.

    Checking by hand. I then checked the moves people know best against cited sources (mostly English Wikipedia articles, and news reports for 2026) and fixed what I found: 45 automatic moves removed (wrong order, wrong item, an institution's seat rather than the capital, duplicates with a different year), 5 corrected, 22 confirmed, and 42 added. Each change is a row in the edits file, with the reason and a link. Where I was not sure, I left Wikidata as it is.

    Measuring. Distances are great-circle distances between the two capitals' coordinates; direction is the initial compass bearing. Each move is placed in the present-day country where most of its state's capitals lie (Natural Earth borders). Distance from the sea is to the nearest ocean coastline in Natural Earth's land polygons (so the Caspian and the great lakes do not count). A move is temporary if the new seat was held for less than a year, if the capital was back where it started within ten years, or if it was a wartime or exile seat (listed by hand).

    Confidence. Every move carries a flag: checked (against a cited source, 69), high (Wikidata dates both ends consistently, 111) or medium (one end dated, 4). High confidence means the record is internally consistent, not that a historian would agree: Wikidata's dates for medieval and early modern states are often rounded or disputed.

    What this is not. It is not a complete history. It counts moves of a state's recorded capital, which for older states often means a royal court or a ruler's residence. It misses moves Wikidata does not record and that I did not add, and it is much richer for Europe than elsewhere. Colonies appear as their own states (German East Africa, British North Borneo), present-day countries only where Wikidata gives them past capitals. The word "capital" also hides real ambiguity: Bolivia, the Netherlands, South Africa, Benin and Malaysia divide the functions of a capital between cities, and some moves (Dodoma, Yamoussoukro, Kotte) moved the title, or parliament, long before the ministries followed.
